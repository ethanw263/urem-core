# KB-0001 — Recognition Disequilibrium

**Status:** Accepted

**Date:** 2026

---

# Problem

The earliest versions of the project focused primarily on identifying exceptional landscapes using physical suitability metrics.

Although useful, this approach could not explain why some highly exceptional locations became well known while others remained largely unrecognized.

The framework lacked a theory explaining the difference between physical potential and observed recognition.

---

# Decision

The project shifted from predicting suitability toward modeling **Recognition Disequilibrium (RDE).**

Recognition Disequilibrium measures the gap between expected recognition and observed recognition.

Rather than asking:

> "Where are the best places?"

the framework now asks:

> "Which places receive substantially less recognition than expected given their characteristics?"

---

# Rationale

This shift provided several important advantages.

It transformed the project from:

- suitability analysis

into

- a theory of geographic recognition.

It also enabled:

- hypothesis testing
- statistical validation
- independent external validation
- mechanism discovery
- mathematical formalization

The framework became explanatory rather than descriptive.

---

# Consequences

This decision fundamentally changed every component of the project.

Expected Recognition became a core model.

Recognition Disequilibrium became the primary quantity of interest.

Validation experiments shifted toward evaluating independent evidence for recognition imbalance.

Future mathematical development now focuses on understanding the mechanisms that produce Recognition Disequilibrium rather than simply ranking locations.

---

# Long-Term Impact

This decision established Recognition Disequilibrium as the central theoretical concept underlying the RDE framework.

Nearly every subsequent architectural and scientific decision builds upon this foundation.
